Three modulations — three hiding places

A carrier y = A sin(2πft + φ) has only three knobs. Twist amplitude A and you get AM, frequency f gives FM, phase φ gives PM/PSK. The three sliders from the Wave Lab map exactly onto the three modulation families.

AM puts the information in the wave's outline (envelope), so receivers are simple (a crystal radio is one diode). But noise also rides the amplitude, so AM is noise-prone. Push the depth past 1 and the envelope folds over and distorts — try it with the slider.

FM keeps amplitude constant; the information lives in the wave's density (frequency). Amplitude noise can be ignored, hence FM radio's clean sound — at the cost of more bandwidth.

PSK is digital: bits switch the phase. BPSK uses 0°/180°, one bit per symbol. Watch closely — the wave snaps into reverse at bit boundaries.

Why GNSS uses PSK (BPSK)

(1) Constant envelope: satellite amplifiers run most efficiently at saturation, which only works when the amplitude carries no information. On a solar-powered satellite, that efficiency matters enormously.

(2) Phase = time: GNSS is fundamentally about time-of-arrival. A modulation that keeps its information in phase doubles as a timing ruler — carrier-phase positioning (RTK) grows straight out of this.

(3) It composes with spreading codes: multiplying by a ±1 PRN code IS BPSK modulation (as in Signal Structure).

Extensions: QPSK carries 2 bits/symbol (L5 uses I/Q channels this way), and BOC modulation (L1C, Galileo E1) shapes the phase flips with a sub-carrier.

Modulation and bandwidth — faster data means fatter signals

A modulated wave spreads into "sidebands" around the carrier: twice the message bandwidth for AM, roughly 2(Δf + fm) for FM (Carson's rule), and roughly the symbol rate for digital schemes. The faster you send, the wider you spread — which is why spectrum is precious.

Same logic in GNSS: the C/A code occupies ~2 MHz while the 10× faster L5 code takes ~20 MHz (compare the widths on the Frequency Band Map).
