What is GNSS?

"I know GPS — but what's GNSS?" The first step into satellite positioning

Your phone's map can show "you are here" because it's receiving radio signals from satellites in space.

Those satellites don't belong to just one country. America's GPS, Europe's Galileo, China's BeiDou, Russia's GLONASS, Japan's QZSS (Michibiki)… several separate "satellite positioning systems" are circling the Earth.

The umbrella term for all of them is GNSS — Global Navigation Satellite System. Your phone actually uses several of them at the same time to work out where you are.

How are GPS and GNSS different?

People often call all satellite positioning "GPS", but GPS is just one of several systems (the American one). The umbrella term for all of them is GNSS. It's like calling every tissue by one brand name.

"Global" = works anywhere on Earth. "Regional" = focused on a specific area (around Japan, or around India). QZSS complements GPS and lingers almost directly overhead in Japan (→ QZSS Michibiki).

So how does it know "you are here"?

Each satellite constantly broadcasts "the exact time now" and "where I am in space". Your receiver (phone) measures how long that signal took to arrive to work out the distance to the satellite. Radio travels at the speed of light, so time gives you distance.

  1. Knowing the distance to one satellite pins you to "somewhere on a sphere around it"
  2. Add a second and third, and the spheres intersect, narrowing the candidates sharply
  3. A fourth satellite also solves your receiver-clock error, fixing a single point in space (latitude, longitude, height)

That's why positioning needs at least four satellites. Try the mechanism → GNSS Signal Simulator; how distance is measured → Pseudorange & Doppler.

Why use several systems?

Today's phones and car navs use not just GPS but Galileo, BeiDou, QZSS… all at once. Seeing more satellites brings a lot of benefits.

Works in cities & mountains

Even if buildings or terrain hide a few satellites, with many systems you can still keep enough "visible" ones.

Faster, steadier fixes

More candidate satellites means a quicker fix after power-on, and a less jittery position.

Better accuracy

The more spread-out satellites you can use, the more firmly the position solves (→ DOP).

Resilience if one fails

If one system has an outage or is jammed, another can cover for it.
