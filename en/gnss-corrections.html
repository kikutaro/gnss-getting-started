Errors & Corrections

Subtracting the known errors from the pseudorange, one by one — from metres to centimetres

INSIDE THE PSEUDORANGE — A CORRECTION IS "SUBTRACT A KNOWN ERROR"

As the Pseudorange page showed, the range ρ a receiver measures is the true geometric distance r plus a stack of errors:

ρ = r + c·δtreceiver − c·δtsatellite + Iiono + Ttropo + multipath + noise

Of these, δtreceiver is solved as an unknown in the position fix (that's why 4 satellites are needed). A correction is subtracting the remaining terms whose value we can figure out. The satellite clock δtsatellite, ionosphere I and troposphere T can each be estimated — from broadcast data, a model, dual-frequency observation, or external augmentation — and removed. Multipath and noise are random errors that simply remain.

WHAT EACH CORRECTION IS — THE ERROR AND HOW IT'S REMOVED

Satellite clock

a few m (far more if uncorrected)

Even the satellite's atomic clock drifts slightly. Ground control monitors it and broadcasts the drift coefficients af0/af1/af2 in the navigation message; the receiver subtracts the clock error. In a 1 µs = 300 m world, this is essential.

Satellite orbit (ephemeris)

1–2 m

The satellite's true position differs slightly from the predicted one. The broadcast ephemeris gives the position, but it carries residual error. Precise orbits (from IGS or CLAS) push this down to centimetres.

Ionosphere

m to tens of m (the biggest source)

The ionosphere delays the signal, and crucially the delay depends on frequency. Single-frequency removes only about half via the broadcast Klobuchar model. Dual-frequency (L1+L5) computes the delay from the difference and removes it almost completely.

Troposphere

2–10 m (larger at low elevation)

Delay from the lower atmosphere (dry air + water vapour). It is frequency-independent, so dual-frequency can't remove it; instead a model (Saastamoinen etc.) plus an elevation mapping function corrects it. The dry part fits well; water vapour is the residual.

Multipath (remains)

tens of cm to a few m

Reflections off buildings and the ground. It's environment-dependent and unpredictable, so no model can correct it. You can only reduce it — antenna design and siting, L5's wide band, carrier smoothing.

Receiver noise (remains)

tens of cm and up

Random measurement scatter from correlation and tracking. Larger for code pseudorange, and orders of magnitude smaller with carrier phase — one reason RTK/PPP reach centimetres.

Relativity (the periodic effect from orbit eccentricity), TGD (inter-signal hardware delay) and Earth rotation (the Sagnac effect) are also corrected, but their formulas are fixed and the residuals tiny, so the simulator above omits them.

Why dual-frequency removes the ionosphere

Ionospheric delay is inversely proportional to the square of frequency (delay ∝ 1/f²). So measuring the same satellite on L1 (1575.42 MHz) and L5 (1176.45 MHz) yields two pseudoranges that differ by exactly the delay difference. Since that difference is caused by the ionospheric delay itself, you can back out the delay and subtract it from both, giving an ionosphere-free range.

A single-frequency receiver can't take that difference, so it must estimate with the broadcast Klobuchar model (a rough functional shape) and removes only about half on average. This is the answer to "why multiple frequencies" from the Frequency Band Map.

Troposphere can't be removed by dual-frequency — so you model it

Tropospheric delay (from the neutral atmosphere, surface to ~a dozen km) is frequency-independent. L1 and L5 are delayed equally, so the dual-frequency difference cancels it out. Instead you estimate the zenith delay with a physical model (Saastamoinen or Hopfield), then map it to the slant direction with an elevation-dependent mapping function.

The delay splits into a "dry component" (~90%), well-determined from surface pressure, and a "wet component" (~10%). Water vapour is the troublesome part — it changes moment to moment and becomes the residual. Turned around, this very property lets GNSS measure atmospheric water vapour, a field called "GNSS meteorology".

The augmentation landscape — from DGPS to PPP-RTK

DGPS: a nearby base station broadcasts "here is the error right now". A classic method that fades with distance.

SBAS (Japan's MSAS, the US WAAS, etc.): geostationary satellites broadcast wide-area corrections (satellite clock/orbit and an ionosphere grid). ~1 m class, a staple of aviation.

RTK: uses a base station's carrier-phase data to resolve the integer ambiguity, reaching centimetres — typically within tens of km of the base.

PPP: no base station, using only precise global satellite clock/orbit corrections. Usable anywhere on Earth, but slow to converge (initialize).

PPP-RTK / CLAS: PPP plus regional atmospheric corrections, for RTK-like accuracy in a short time. QZSS's CLAS is Japan's implementation that broadcasts it straight from the satellite on L6, giving centimetres with no base-station link.

All of this feeds directly into the "corrections" block of the Receive Chain. Which augmentation you use takes the same receiver from metres to centimetres.
