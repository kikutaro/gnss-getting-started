LEO-PNT — low-earth-orbit positioning

Positioning from satellites flying far lower than GNSS. Strong signal, jam-resistant — the leading next-gen PNT

The idea — put the satellites much lower

GNSS satellites like GPS orbit at about 20,200 km (medium earth orbit, MEO). From that far away the signal is extremely weak at the ground and becomes a target for jamming and spoofing. So we flip it: place positioning satellites in low earth orbit (LEO), a few hundred to ~1,500 km up. That's LEO-PNT — about the same height Starlink flies at.

Why is closer better? The shorter distance makes the signal orders of magnitude stronger, so it reaches indoors more easily and is harder to jam. And LEO satellites race across the sky, so the geometry keeps changing, positioning solutions converge fast, and spoofing is easier to catch. Pros and cons

Strong signal

Distance is 1/20–1/40 → hundreds to thousands× the power. Reaches indoors more easily.

Jam/spoof resistant

A strong signal is hard to drown out; fast geometry change and many satellites make spoofing easy to detect.

Fast convergence

Satellites move quickly and geometry changes fast, so a precise solution is reached sooner.

Large Doppler

High speed means big frequency shifts — usable as a positioning cue in itself.

Needs many satellites

Each is visible only briefly, so global coverage needs a constellation of hundreds.

Orbit maintenance

Low orbits decay from atmospheric drag; regular station-keeping and replacement are needed.

Still being built out

Dedicated LEO-PNT is at the demo/deployment stage; GNSS-level maturity is yet to come.

Reference time still needed

Managing satellite clocks and orbits is essential; many designs work with GNSS and ground stations.

Why it resists jamming/spoofing — not just "strength"

LEO-PNT's resilience isn't decided by signal strength alone; several reasons stack up.

① Strong signal

Orders of magnitude stronger because it's close — hard to drown out with jamming.

② Fast-changing geometry · many sats

Satellites move fast with large Doppler, so keeping a consistent fake going is hard, and many satellites cross-check each other.

③ Signal spec is non-public · authenticated

Many LEO-PNT signals don't publish their spec and add cryptographic authentication, so an attacker can't forge a convincing signal.

④ Independent of GNSS

A separate physics and system. Breaking it and GNSS at once is extremely hard; each backs up the other.

Point ③ is the big difference from GNSS. GNSS civil signals are fully published for interoperability (ICDs), so anyone can synthesize a matching signal — which is why spoofing is feasible. Many LEO-PNT signals, by contrast, keep the spec private and add cryptographic authentication, making spoofing difficult. Note, though, that the real strength is the authentication, not mere secrecy (GNSS is fighting back too, e.g. QZSS signal authentication).

Main efforts

dedicated · in demo

Xona Space Systems (PULSAR)

Building a dedicated LEO positioning constellation. GPS-like thinking aimed at a stronger, authenticated signal. Demonstration satellites already launched.

US · dedicated

TrustPoint

A US startup building a commercial, dedicated LEO-PNT service. Encrypted, authenticated signals aim at jam- and spoof-resistance.

commercial · operating

Iridium STL (Satelles)

Broadcasts a time/position signal (STL) from the existing 66 Iridium LEO satellites. Indoor penetration and jam-resistance are its strengths; already used to back up time sync.

China · private

CENTISPACE (China)

China's private effort (Future Navigation) for a LEO positioning-augmentation constellation. Demonstration satellites already launched.

China · state

China SatNet (Guowang)

China's state-owned mega-constellation. Mainly communications, but a national project with PNT in view.

planned · demo

ESA LEO-PNT / national programs

Europe (ESA) plans in-orbit demonstrations; China and others envision PNT augmentation with large LEO constellations. A worldwide trend to complement GNSS.

Japan · research/demo

JAXA / ArkEdge Space

Japan is moving on LEO-PNT too. JAXA is researching next-generation positioning, and the microsatellite startup ArkEdge Space has announced work toward positioning with small-satellite constellations. The aim is resilient PNT combined with the existing QZSS (Michibiki).

Korea · agencies

KARI / KASA (Korea)

South Korea's space agencies — KARI (Korea Aerospace Research Institute) and the new KASA (est. 2024) — are pushing LEO-PNT research and deployment, alongside the regional KPS.

repurposing · research

Repurposing Starlink, etc.

Active research also uses communications LEO (Starlink/OneWeb) signals for positioning. See the Signals of Opportunity page.
