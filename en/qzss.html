WHY A FIGURE-8?

Orbital elementQZSS valueEffect
Period~23 h 56 minSynchronous with Earth's rotation → returns to the same place daily (the track closes)
Inclination i~41°Swings north and south of the equator → the vertical extent of the 8
Eccentricity e~0.075Elliptical: slow at apogee → the asymmetry between the two lobes
Arg. of perigee ω~270°Puts apogee over the northern hemisphere → longer dwell above Japan

A geostationary satellite (zero inclination) appears frozen in the sky. Tilt the orbit and the satellite travels north and south of the equator once a day, tracing a figure-8 as seen from the ground. QZSS goes further and makes the orbit elliptical, so the satellite crawls through the northern lobe and hurries through the southern one — maximising the time spent directly above Japan. Each satellite covers Japan for roughly 8 hours.

WHY HIGH ELEVATION MATTERS

You cannot choose where GPS satellites sit. Low-elevation ones hide behind buildings and hills and pick up more multipath. QZSS is almost overhead, so:

Strong in urban canyons

Even on a street hemmed in by towers, the zenith stays visible — extra satellites where you need them most.

Mountains and forests

Slopes and canopy block the horizon first; the zenith is the last direction to be lost.

Less multipath

A signal from straight above has a short reflected path, so it contributes little error.

Less atmosphere

The lower the elevation, the longer the path through ionosphere and troposphere. The zenith is the shortest route.

QZSS'S OWN SERVICES

L1C/A · L1C · L2C · L5

GPS-compatible positioning signals

GPS-compatible signals on PRN 193+. To a receiver it is simply "more GPS satellites" — no modification needed. The navigation message is GPS-compatible too.

L1S

Sub-meter augmentation (SLAS)

Broadcasts error estimates derived from a network of reference stations, improving standalone positioning to the ~1 m level. Same frequency as L1, so the antenna and RF front-end work unchanged.

L1S

Disaster & crisis reports (DC Report)

Broadcasts disaster-prevention alerts (earthquakes, tsunamis, eruptions, weather warnings) straight from the satellite — a public service beyond positioning that reaches you even when the cellular network is down or congested.

L6D

Centimeter augmentation (CLAS)

Broadcasts PPP-RTK corrections for few-cm positioning — with no data subscription to a reference-station service. For automated driving, agriculture and surveying.

L6E

MADOCA-PPP

Distributes PPP corrections across a wide Asia-Oceania area — broader coverage than CLAS (at the cost of longer convergence time).

S band

Safety confirmation (Q-ANPI)

In a disaster, shelters can send status reports via the satellite (an uplink). A rare two-way GNSS service that gets information out even when terrestrial links have failed.

auth

Signal authentication

Adds a digital signature to the broadcast signals and corrections, so a receiver can verify "did the genuine Michibiki send this?" — a direct countermeasure to spoofing.

QZSS and NavIC are both regional systems, but they place their satellites differently. QZSS uses quasi-zenith orbits (the tilted figure-8) to buy zenith visibility, whereas NavIC uses a mix of geostationary and inclined-geosynchronous satellites over India, with its own frequency plan on L5 and S band (see the frequency band map). Choosing "generous over home" instead of "even worldwide" gets you a big effect from few satellites — the design philosophy of a regional system.

"Complement" and "augment" — QZSS's two roles

Complement: by emitting the same signal format as GPS, it simply "adds visible satellites". When GPS alone falls short of four in a city, a QZSS satellite overhead makes a fix possible. It is easy for receivers to support, so most modern phones already do.

Augmentation: distributing error corrections. This is L1S SLAS (~1 m) and L6 CLAS (cm-level). High-precision positioning normally needs a data link (cellular) to receive reference-station data, but QZSS broadcasts it straight from the satellite, so high accuracy works even in out-of-coverage mountains or at sea.

This "complement + augment" pairing is exactly what a small regional constellation makes possible: freed from covering the whole planet, it can stack the functions Japan actually needs.

The quasi-zenith design in numbers

Match the period to a sidereal day (23 h 56 min 4 s) and the satellite locks step with Earth's rotation. In the Kepler terms from the coordinates page, the semi-major axis a ≈ 42,164 km (same as geostationary).

Zero inclination would give a geostationary satellite; tilt it 41° and the satellite travels north and south of the equator once a day. The ground track becomes a figure-8 from the interplay of that north-south motion with Earth's rotation. Make the orbit elliptical (e = 0.075) with apogee — the slow point — in the northern hemisphere, and the northern lobe stretches out, keeping the satellite lingering over Japan.

The upshot: each satellite spends about 8 hours a day high over Japan. Space three of them 8 hours apart and you get "one at the zenith" around the clock — which is why the three satellites chase the same figure-8 in the ground track on the left.

Growing the constellation, and what's next

QZSS began with the first "Michibiki" launch (2010) and started service with four satellites in 2018 — currently three in quasi-zenith orbit plus one geostationary. Expansion to a seven-satellite constellation is underway, with concepts for growing further still.

Why seven matters: with today's four, QZSS cannot fix a position on its own when GPS is unavailable (a fix needs at least four satellites — see the pseudorange page). Seven brings Japan close to always having 4+ QZSS satellites in view, opening the door to positioning by QZSS alone (a sustainable, independent capability) — which carries real significance for having a positioning backbone that does not depend on another country's system.
