A traditional radio has its demodulator baked into hardware — an "FM radio", a "GPS chip". SDR shrinks the hardware to the minimum (frequency conversion + AD/DA) and does the rest in software. The same board becomes an FM radio, an air-band scanner, or a GNSS receiver / GNSS signal generator just by changing code. The acquisition-tracking-decoding chain from the Receive Chain page can run on your own PC.

What are I/Q samples? — SDR's common language

What an SDR emits (and consumes) is a stream of "I/Q samples" — complex numbers. Relative to the carrier, a signal is described by its in-phase component I (the cos side) and quadrature component Q (the sin side). It's exactly the rotating phasor from Wave Basics: I and Q are the arrow's X and Y. Amplitude is √(I²+Q²), phase is atan2(Q, I).

With I/Q you can recover amplitude, frequency and phase afterwards — which is why AM/FM/PSK can all be demodulated in software. The "I/Q file" that a tool like gps-sdr-sim generates is a GNSS waveform recorded in this format.

Bit depth (8 vs 12 bit) — dynamic range

Each ADC bit buys ~6 dB of dynamic range: ~48 dB at 8 bits, ~72 dB at 12. In dB-page terms it's "how far apart can the strongest and weakest signals be and still coexist".

GNSS digs its signal out from below the noise by correlation, so coarse quantization actually works (dedicated chips use 1–3 bits!). But with a strong nearby transmitter (a cell tower), 8 bits get eaten by the strong signal and the faint GNSS gets pushed out. 12-bit boards are more forgiving.

Reference clock accuracy — ppm decides GNSS usability

An SDR's frequency comes from its reference oscillator (XO/TCXO/VCTCXO), with error stated in ppm. At L1 = 1575.42 MHz, 1 ppm of error = ~1.58 kHz of frequency offset. The acquisition search expects roughly ±5 kHz of Doppler, so a bare ±20 ppm HackRF (≈±31 kHz) won't find satellites without widening the search.

LimeSDR boards are popular for GNSS precisely because their VCTCXOs (±0.5–1 ppm class) keep this small. Conversely, a TCXO-equipped RTL-SDR is plenty for L1 reception. Serious setups discipline everything with an external 10 MHz reference (GPSDO).

GNSS × SDR — the classic tools

Receive: GNSS-SDR (a full open-source GNSS receiver — acquisition, tracking, decoding and PVT all in software), PocketSDR (a favorite among GNSS researchers). An RTL-SDR plus a patch antenna is enough to watch L1 C/A acquisition and tracking happen. Seeing each block of the Receive Chain as actual code is quite a moment.

Transmit (signal generation): gps-sdr-sim builds an I/Q file of GPS L1 C/A for any position and time, which an SDR replays — the standard way to test receivers. But radiating into the air is illegal in most countries; always use a cable plus attenuators. The next page, Transmitting GNSS with SDR, covers designing the right signal level with LimeSDR boards.
