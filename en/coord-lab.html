Ellipsoidal height h (raw GNSS height) = elevation H (map / leveling height) + geoid height N. Around Japan N ≈ +30–40 m, so a phone's raw GPS altitude reads ~30–40 m above the map elevation unless the app corrects it.

ECEF (Earth-Centered, Earth-Fixed) Cartesian coordinates: origin at Earth's center of mass, X toward longitude 0°, Z toward the north pole. Satellite positions and the PVT solution are all computed in XYZ, then converted to lat/lon/height for display. Defaults: near Tokyo Station.

What is the geoid? — "sea level extended under the land"

The geoid is the surface you'd get by extending mean sea level beneath the continents — an equipotential surface of gravity (water doesn't flow along it). Reflecting the lumpy mass distribution inside the Earth, it undulates by up to ±100 m against the smooth ellipsoid. Elevation (height above sea level) is measured from the geoid, which is the practical height that agrees with which way water flows.

GNSS, being pure geometry, outputs height above the ellipsoid h. The difference is the geoid height N: h = H + N. Receivers and map apps convert to H using a geoid model (in Japan, the GSI geoid model). When "GPS altitude looks wrong", the culprit is almost always whether N was corrected.

Datums — the same spot doesn't have just one coordinate

Latitude and longitude only mean something once you fix a convention (datum): which ellipsoid, positioned where. The GNSS world standard is WGS84 (the GPS datum), consistent with the international ITRF at the cm level. Japan's official JGD2011 is also ITRF-based and practically identical to WGS84.

A history lesson: the old Tokyo Datum, used until 2002, was based on 19th-century astronomical surveys and disagreed with WGS84 by ~400 m — a famous source of old-map offsets and car-nav conversion bugs. And since plates move, Japan drifts several cm per year; precision surveying even has to state the epoch ("coordinates as of when").

The coordinate pipeline inside a receiver

① Compute each satellite's position in ECEF XYZ from the ephemeris → ② solve the pseudorange equations to get the receiver's position, also in ECEF XYZ → ③ convert to lat/lon/ellipsoidal height with the ellipsoid formulas → ④ apply the geoid model to show elevation.

NMEA's GGA sentence carries "elevation + geoid height" as separate fields rather than ellipsoidal height (see Receive Chain). Handy mental conversions for position errors: 1 arc-second of latitude ≈ 31 m; 1 arc-second of longitude ≈ 25 m around Tokyo's latitude.
