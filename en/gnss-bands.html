The main event — why bands cluster at the same frequencies

Watch 1575.42 MHz (L1/E1/B1C) and 1176.45 MHz (L5/E5a/B2a). GPS, QZSS, Galileo, BeiDou and NavIC all put signals at the same center frequencies. Not a coincidence — it is international coordination for interoperability. One antenna and one RF front-end can receive several systems at once, making cheap multi-GNSS receivers possible. Your phone's GNSS chip is exactly this.

GLONASS G1/G2 sit apart at their own frequencies, using FDMA (each satellite on a slightly different frequency; the newer CDMA G3 moves closer to the others). BeiDou's B3, Galileo's E6 and QZSS's L6 are system-specific bands used for differentiated services such as high-accuracy corrections.

Why L band (1.1–1.6 GHz) at all?

Nearly all satellite navigation lives in L band because it balances everything: lower frequencies suffer more from the ionosphere (error and scintillation), higher frequencies attenuate more in rain and need trickier antennas. L band has modest ionospheric effects, negligible weather loss, and compact antennas — the sweet spot.

The regions around L1 and L5 are also protected ARNS (Aeronautical Radio Navigation Service) allocations, keeping interference regulated — important for safety-of-life uses like aviation.

FDMA vs CDMA — why GLONASS looks different

Most GNSS use CDMA: every satellite transmits on the same center frequency, distinguished by different spreading codes (PRN codes). Classic GLONASS uses FDMA: same code, but each satellite offset by ~0.5625 MHz. That's why the G1/G2 bars are a bit wide — they are rows of per-satellite channels.

FDMA complicates receiver RF design and hinders interoperability, so modern GLONASS adds CDMA signals (G3 and others).
