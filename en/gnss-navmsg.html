    Ephemeris vs almanac

    The ephemeris is that satellite's own precise orbit (Keplerian elements + corrections). It is valid only 2–4 hours and rebroadcast constantly — the primary data for computing satellite position in a fix.

    The almanac is a coarse list of every satellite's orbit and health. Low precision but usable for weeks to months, it lets a receiver predict which satellites should be overhead and speeds up acquisition. Collecting all 25 LNAV pages takes 12.5 minutes — one reason old receivers took forever for a first fix (today A-GNSS fills in over the network).

    The evolution: LNAV → CNAV → CNAV-2

    LNAV (L1 C/A, since 1978) uses fixed 300-bit subframes of ten 30-bit words, each guarded by 6-bit Hamming parity. CNAV (L2C/L5) drops the fixed frame for flexible "message types" and upgrades error detection to CRC-24. Orbits are sent as a delta from a reference (ΔA) instead of √A, improving precision.

    CNAV-2 (L1C) goes further: three different-length subframes — a 9-bit TOI, 600 bits of clock+ephemeris and a 274-bit page — protected by BCH and LDPC codes for weak-signal reception. Switch formats above to compare words+parity vs message+CRC vs variable-length subframes.

    Note that L1 C/A LNAV remains the lingua franca every satellite still speaks; newer signals are additive upgrades.

    Data rates and a sense of time

    LNAV runs at 50 bps: 6 s per 300-bit subframe, 18 s for a full ephemeris (SF1–3). CNAV is 25 bps on L2C (12 s per message) and 50 bps on L5 (6 s). CNAV-2 uses 100 sps and an 18 s frame.

    These are absurdly slow links — by design, so the signal survives arriving 20,000 km later below the noise floor. And the time mark at the head of every subframe/message (TOW/TOI) is the reference for pseudorange measurement: the heart of GNSS.
