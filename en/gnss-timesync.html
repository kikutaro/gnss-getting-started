Time Synchronization — GNSS distributes "time"

Why time keeps coming up in positioning — the atomic clocks aboard satellites, and GNSS's hidden role in keeping the world in sync

Why does "positioning" talk so much about "time"?

At its heart, GNSS positioning is about measuring how long a signal takes to arrive from a satellite. Radio travels at the speed of light (~300,000 km/s), so time gives you distance. Distance = speed of light × travel time is the core of positioning (→ Pseudorange page).

1 slight travels ~300,000 km (7.5× around Earth)
1 µs= one millionth of a second → ~300 m
1 ns= one billionth of a second → ~30 cm

So a clock that is off by just one nanosecond means ~30 cm of range error. To reach metre-level positioning, a satellite's clock must be accurate to the nanosecond. That's why GNSS is a satellite-positioning system and, at the same time, a system of ultra-precise clocks (time basics → Time & Clocks).

The atomic clocks aboard satellites

Quartz isn't good enough, so GNSS satellites carry atomic clocks. Using the way atoms absorb and emit electromagnetic waves at one specific frequency as a "pendulum" gives a clock that barely drifts with temperature or age. Most satellites carry several units for redundancy, switching if one degrades.

Today the "second" is defined as the time for 9,192,631,770 oscillations of the radiation from a caesium atom. (Note: "Iridium" is a satellite-phone network, not an atomic clock.) Even atomic clocks drift slightly, so the ground control estimates the error and broadcasts clock corrections (→ Errors & Corrections, Navigation Messages).

GNSS time scales — GPS time vs UTC and leap seconds

From the unbroken time their atomic clocks keep, each GNSS builds its own time scale. This is where leap seconds come in. The UTC (Coordinated Universal Time) we live by occasionally adds a second to track Earth's rotation, while GPS time is a continuous scale with no leap seconds. So the two slowly diverge.

TAI (Int'l Atomic Time)

Atomic clocks combined; no leap seconds. The foundation. reference

GPS time

Epoch 1980-01-06, no leap seconds. = TAI − 19 s (fixed)

UTC

Follows Earth's rotation via leap seconds. = TAI − 37 s (as of 2026)

As a result, GPS time − UTC = 18 s (since 2017). This offset (the leap-second info) is broadcast in the navigation message, so a receiver can convert GPS time to correct UTC. Devices that use the time get an accurate, UTC-traceable time through this conversion.

Time-sync use cases — GNSS as a "time distributor"

A GNSS receiver outputs not just position but a one-per-second pulse (1PPS) and UTC time. Much of our infrastructure uses this as its time source to stay in sync.

📱

Mobile network base-station sync

5G and LTE-TDD split uplink/downlink in time to share one frequency, so base stations must agree on time to the microsecond. Drift causes interference with neighbouring cells and disrupts handover. Many base stations use GNSS 1PPS as their reference clock.

need ~1.5 µs
⚡

Power-grid phasor measurement (PMU)

Wide-area monitoring measures voltage/current phase at the same instant across the grid (WAMS). Meters scattered nationwide time-stamp with microsecond GNSS time so their phases can be compared.

need ~1 µs
💹

Financial trade timestamps

High-frequency trading is required by regulation to record order/execution times traceable to UTC (e.g. Europe's MiFID II). Exchanges and servers sync to GNSS time to keep trade ordering exact.

need ~100 µs
🖥️

Network & data-centre time source

The top NTP/PTP servers (Stratum-1 / grandmaster) use a GNSS receiver as their time source. Distributed databases use time uncertainty to keep ordering and consistency across far-apart servers.

Digital broadcast (single-frequency network, SFN)

When several transmitters send the same signal on the same frequency at the same instant, coverage becomes wide and seamless. GNSS time aligns that "same instant".

Science & geodesy

VLBI linking distant telescopes, nationwide seismometers, and astronomy only make sense when remote records are combined on a common time. GNSS time is that ruler.

The risk of over-relying on GNSS — holdover and interference

With so much infrastructure depending on GNSS time, the problem is when GNSS goes away. A device that loses reception enters "holdover", keeping time on its internal clock alone — but that clock (often quartz or rubidium) slowly drifts, as the comparison above shows. Within hours to days it can exceed a microsecond of tolerance.

And because GNSS signals are extremely weak, they can be targets for jamming or spoofing (feeding a fake time). Timing infrastructure needs defences such as using multiple GNSS, backing up with ground time sources, and anomaly detection (→ Jamming & Spoofing).
