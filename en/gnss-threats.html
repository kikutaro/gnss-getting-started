Jamming, Spoofing & Meaconing

Three ways to fool GNSS — what happens inside the receiver, and how to catch each one

⚠ Where this page stands: this is about detecting and defending against these attacks. Radiating in GNSS bands (jamming or transmitting fake signals) is illegal in virtually every country, and deceiving someone else's receiver is a crime. Any experiment belongs on a cable with attenuators, or inside a shield box, against your own equipment only (see Transmitting GNSS with SDR).

Jamming

DENIAL

Floods the GNSS band with noise so the real signal is buried under interference. The receiver loses every satellite.

Difficulty Low (cheap illegal jammers exist)
Goal deny positioning
Symptom all C/N₀ drop together → no fix

Spoofing

DECEPTION

Fabricates convincing satellite signals so the receiver believes a false position and time.

Difficulty High (needs signal generation and alignment)
Goal make the victim trust a lie
Symptom looks healthy, but the position is wrong

Meaconing

REBROADCAST

Receives genuine signals elsewhere and rebroadcasts them with a delay. The signals are authentic, so they're hard to reject.

Difficulty Medium (receive, amplify, re-radiate)
Goal move the victim to another place/time
Symptom position collapses onto the relay antenna

    One giveaway is shared by all three: everything arrives from a single direction. Real satellites are scattered across the sky; an attack comes out of one antenna — which is exactly what multi-element antennas (CRPA) and attitude-aware receivers exploit.

    Why GNSS is such an easy target

    As the dB page showed, GNSS arrives at about −130 dBm — more than 20 dB below the thermal noise floor. It only works thanks to correlation gain, so polluting the input is physically cheap: a few watts of interference can silence receivers for kilometres.

    On top of that, civil GPS L1 C/A is publicly specified and unencrypted. Anyone can generate the same waveform, which is what makes spoofing possible at all (the military P(Y) code is encrypted and far harder to fake). Weak and open — the structural price of a free, universal utility.

    What makes meaconing special — rebroadcasting the real thing

    Meaconing fabricates nothing. It picks up genuine GNSS signals in one place (say, outdoors), amplifies them and re-radiates them somewhere else (say, indoors). Because the content is authentic, even cryptographic authentication passes — which is what makes it nasty.

    The outcome is pure geometry: every satellite's signal now travels a common extra path through the relay, so the receiver concludes it is standing at the relay antenna, with its clock late by the delay. The "GPS repeater" sold to bring signals indoors is exactly this mechanism — and it is illegal in many countries, including Japan.

    The flip side: it is detectable. A receiver that should be moving reports a position that never budges, and its clock sits at a constant offset.

    Defences — what receivers and systems can do

    1. Monitor and notice (cheap, immediate): log AGC gain and C/N₀ continuously. Interference makes AGC dive and drops every satellite at once — that alone is a solid anomaly detector. Watch for position/time discontinuities too.

    2. Overwhelm with redundancy: multi-GNSS (GPS + QZSS + Galileo + BeiDou) and multi-frequency (L1 + L5). Faking all of them consistently is dramatically harder, and disagreement between them is itself the alarm. The spread you saw on the band map is resilience in disguise.

    3. Reject it spatially: a controlled reception pattern antenna (CRPA) steers a null toward "the strong thing coming from one direction". Standard in aviation and defence; cheaper cousins include choke rings and simply siting the antenna where the horizon is blocked.

    4. Cross-check other sensors: IMU, odometry, barometric altitude. "GNSS says we moved a kilometre, the IMU says we're parked" collapses the lie instantly — sensor fusion of this kind is standard in aviation and automated driving.

    5. Signal authentication (the real answer): the satellite signs its data. Galileo's OSNMA (navigation message authentication) is operational, GPS has Chimera in development, and Japan's QZSS offers a signal authentication service. It cryptographically proves "a genuine satellite produced this message" — the structural fix for spoofing. (It cannot stop meaconing, whose signals are genuine; pair it with defence 4.)

    Real-world incidents and why the stakes are wide

    GNSS interference is an operational reality. Aviation authorities report GPS loss near airports and widespread spoofing around conflict zones (ships displayed far from their true position, aircraft navigation alerts), and ICAO has issued warnings. Reports of GNSS interference in civil aviation have been trending upward in recent years.

    The impact goes far beyond maps. As the Time & Clocks page showed, cellular networks, power grids and financial systems depend on GNSS time. Losing GNSS is less "my map broke" than "the infrastructure's clocks drift" — which is why countries debate terrestrial backups (eLoran) and high-stability holdover clocks.

    Closer to home, cheap personal privacy jammers (used to defeat vehicle tracking) are a recurring problem: even tiny output disrupts receivers for hundreds of metres to kilometres, and one famously interfered with an airport augmentation system (the Newark case in the US). Owning or using one is illegal in most jurisdictions.
