Terrestrial RF (eLoran, etc.)

From ground stations, not satellites. Powerful long waves — jam-resistant, and a backup for time distribution too

The idea — broadcast from the ground, not from above

GNSS's weakness comes from being "a faint signal arriving from deep space". So flip it: broadcast a strong signal from a high-power ground station. That's the idea of terrestrial PNT. The classic example is eLoran (enhanced Loran), a modernization of the military Loran-C that uses long waves around 100 kHz.

Because long waves are such a low frequency, they travel far and bend around ground and buildings, reaching places GNSS struggles with. And the transmit power is enormous, so it's hard to drown out with jamming — its biggest appeal.

Why it makes a good partner for GNSS

Jam-resistant

High power + low frequency. Its physics is utterly unlike GNSS, so jamming both at once is hard.

Independent

A separate system with zero dependence on satellites — unaffected by space weather or satellite failures.

Distributes time too

Stations sync via atomic clocks; they can broadcast ns-class time/frequency over a wide area, backing up time synchronization.

Wide coverage

One station covers hundreds of km, so few stations can blanket a country.

Position accuracy is roughly 10–20 m, short of GNSS's few metres. But for its value as an "independent, hard-to-jam" system, countries are studying or partly operating it as a PNT backbone for critical infrastructure (UK trials, deployment in South Korea, etc.).

Other terrestrial systems

Time-signal stations (JJY, etc.)

Long-wave standard-time broadcasts. Can't fix position, but distribute accurate time over a wide area — familiar from radio clocks.

Broadcast/telecom sync signals

Digital broadcast and telecom networks synchronize themselves; sometimes usable as a ground time source.

Cellular network positioning

Cell-ID and time-difference positioning from base stations is also terrestrial. 5G standardizes positioning (see Signals of Opportunity).

Pseudolites

Ground-based "GPS transmitters" augmenting a local area (airport, indoors) with GNSS-compatible signals.

The downsides: it needs ground infrastructure (transmitters) built and maintained, its accuracy is below GNSS, and terrain/RF conditions affect it. So the realistic role is an independent backup used alongside GNSS, not a replacement. Big picture: GNSS Alternatives (overview).
