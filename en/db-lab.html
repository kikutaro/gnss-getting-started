Just how weak is −130 dBm? Learn the decibel — the language of signal strength — by feel

START HERE — dB IS A WAY OF COUNTING "HOW MANY TIMES"

Signal strength is measured as power (watts). And in the radio world, strength changes by multiplication, not addition: an amplifier makes it ×100, a long cable ×1/2, a trip through space ×1/1,000,000,000,000. Doing that multiplication all day is painful, so engineers invented a notation that counts the multiplier in orders of magnitude. That's the decibel (dB).

You only need two facts: ×10 = +10 dB and ×2 ≈ +3 dB (so ×1/2 ≈ −3 dB, ×1/10 = −10 dB). Chain some amplifiers and attenuators below and watch multiplication turn into plain addition.

The dB rules — why multiplication becomes addition

Signal power spans 16 orders of magnitude from transmit (tens of watts) to receive (10⁻¹⁶ W). Logarithms tame that multiplicative world: define dB = 10 log₁₀(ratio) and "one millionth" becomes "−60 dB", three ×2 amplifier stages become "+3+3+3 = +9 dB" — every gain and loss is just addition. That's why the link-budget chart steps neatly downward.

Memorize just two facts: +10 dB = ×10, +3 dB ≈ ×2. Combine them for anything (e.g. +26 dB = 10×10×2×2 = ×400).

Thermal noise kTB — the floor that exists no matter what

Electrons jiggling with heat in any resistor generate noise power N = kTB (k: Boltzmann constant, T: temperature, B: bandwidth). At room temperature (290 K) that is −174 dBm/Hz — a floor set by physics. Wider bandwidth means more noise: in the C/A signal's 2 MHz, −174 + 63 = ≈−111 dBm.

GPS arrives at −130 dBm, about 20 dB below that floor — a spectrum analyzer shows nothing at all. Reception works only because correlation provides ~43 dB of processing gain (see Signal Structure). Digging signals out from under the noise is the essence of GNSS reception.

C/N₀ vs SNR — reading the receiver's bar graph

GNSS convention states signal quality as C/N₀ (dB-Hz) rather than SNR, because it doesn't depend on bandwidth choice: SNR = C/N₀ − 10log₁₀(B). The per-satellite bars in u-center are C/N₀.

Rules of thumb: 45+ dB-Hz = good open-sky reception, 35–45 = normal, 28–35 = weak (urban, window), below 28 = tracking starts to drop. Crank up the indoor loss above and find that limit yourself.
