WHAT TO NOTICE ABOUT GPS ON A SPECTRUM

The signal is buried in noise

GPS at the ground is about −130 dBm, roughly 20 dB below the thermal noise floor (~−111 dBm). Look normally and you can't tell it from noise — as on the dB page.

A gentle trapezoid, not a spike

A spread-code (BPSK) spectrum has a sinc² shape. The C/A code is a soft hump about 2 MHz wide — nothing like the sharp peak of an FM station.

Signals of different widths overlap

L1 stacks the narrow C/A (±1 MHz) and the wider P(Y) (±10 MHz) on the same center frequency. You see a broad skirt sitting under the narrow hump.

Seeing it takes effort

Because it sinks into noise, in practice you need long averaging, or the receiver's post-correlation processing gain, before any hump appears.

The faster the chip rate, the wider the spectrum spreads (see Signal Structure and the Frequency Band Map). L1C's BOC modulation pushes power away from the center into two side humps, letting it coexist on the same frequency as C/A.

Why a spread signal has a sinc² shape

A spectrum (power vs frequency) is the Fourier transform of the time waveform. The GPS C/A code is a train of square chips of width Tc ≈ 0.98 µs (BPSK). The Fourier transform of one square pulse is the sinc function (sin(x)/x), and power is its square, sinc².

Its main lobe (the central hump) is "twice the chip rate" wide ≈ 2 MHz. The "faster chips → wider band" from Signal Structure shows up here as "the trapezoid skirt spreads sideways". It isn't a sharp peak precisely because the information is deliberately smeared across a wide band (spread spectrum) instead of concentrated at one frequency.

How to visualize a signal buried in noise

On a single sweep, GPS is completely hidden in the noise. To actually see a hump:

1. Long averaging (trace averaging, narrow RBW): random noise averages flat, so only the always-same-shaped signal component faintly emerges — still only a few dB.

2. View it after correlation: multiply by the known PRN code (despread) as a receiver does, and the wide, faint signal folds back onto the center frequency into a sharp peak. That is the ~43 dB processing gain, and it is exactly the correlation peak on the Pseudorange page.

So "raw on a spectrum analyzer" and "correlated in a receiver" are completely different pictures: the first a hint of a trapezoid, the second a sharp peak.

Jamming and interference show up too

A spectrum analyzer is also used for GNSS health checks. If a jammer is nearby, an unnaturally strong feature appears in the GNSS band (a sharp peak for a continuous-wave jammer, a moving hump for a sweep jammer). You notice "something strong is riding on" the otherwise flat noise floor. Airports and reference stations use exactly this kind of spectrum monitoring to catch interference early.
