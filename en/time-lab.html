The definition of "one second" and how atomic clocks work

Today's second is defined by atoms, not astronomy: the time for 9,192,631,770 oscillations of cesium-133. An atomic transition frequency is the ultimate ruler — identical no matter who measures it, where. An atomic clock continuously disciplines a crystal oscillator against that resonance, reaching stabilities of 10⁻¹³–10⁻¹⁵ (one second in millions of years).

GNSS satellites carry several cesium/rubidium clocks. Still imperfect — so their drift coefficients (af0/af1/af2) are broadcast in the navigation message, monitored and uploaded daily by the control segment.

Relativity and GPS — 11 km/day wrong without it

Two relativistic effects act on a GPS satellite clock. Special relativity: moving at 3.9 km/s, it ticks slower than ground clocks (≈−7 μs/day). General relativity: at 20,000 km altitude gravity is weaker, so it ticks faster (≈+45 μs/day). Net: +38 μs/day — about 11 km of range error per day.

So GPS clocks are deliberately tuned before launch: the 10.23 MHz reference is set ~0.00455 Hz low, so that in orbit relativity brings it to exactly 10.23 MHz as seen from the ground. The most famous everyday application of relativity there is.

GPS time, UTC and leap seconds

UTC is atomic time (TAI) with leap seconds inserted to track Earth's wobbly rotation. GPS time starts at Jan 6, 1980 and never adds leap seconds. It began 19 s behind TAI, so always TAI − GPS = 19 s; accumulated leap seconds make GPS − UTC = 18 s today.

Upcoming leap seconds are announced in the navigation message (LNAV SF4's ΔtLS/ΔtLSF; CNAV MT33). Incidentally, leap seconds are internationally agreed to be abolished by 2035 due to operational headaches.

Why a cheap receiver clock is fine (recap)

As the race shows, a receiver TCXO drifts about a microsecond — 300 m — per second. Positioning still works because the clock error is common to every satellite's pseudorange, so it is solved as a fourth unknown alongside position. The receiver gets away with a cheap crystal, and as a bonus ends up knowing time as accurately as an atomic clock — which is why GNSS also serves as timing infrastructure for cell networks, power grids and financial timestamps.
