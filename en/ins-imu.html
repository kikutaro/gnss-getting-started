Inertial Navigation (INS / IMU)

Know your position by integrating your own motion, with no radio at all. The "last line of defence" during a GNSS outage

Position without radio — dead reckoning

An inertial navigation system (INS) works out your position, velocity and attitude from accelerometers (how hard you accelerated) and gyroscopes (how much you turned) alone. The unit bundling these sensors is an IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit). As long as you know the starting point, you just keep adding up the motion — no radio, no satellites, no base stations.

⬆ Accelerometer

Measures acceleration; integrate once for velocity, again for distance travelled.

🌀 Gyroscope

Measures rotation (angular rate); integrate to know "which way you now face" (attitude).

Its greatest strength is being fully self-contained. Receiving no external signal, it is immune to jamming and spoofing in principle (Threats page). Submarines, aircraft, missiles and rockets have used this venerable method for decades.

The weakness is drift — error grows with time

The price of being self-contained is drift. Because you integrate acceleration twice, a tiny sensor bias swells into position error as the square of time. Do nothing after GNSS drops out and the error keeps growing. The speed depends dramatically on the sensor "grade". Move the slider below to change the time since GNSS was lost.

A simplified model: position error ≈ ½ × accelerometer bias × time² (representative teaching values). In reality gyro error and mis-projected gravity also contribute, growing more intricately. The point: the cheaper the sensor, the faster it drifts away.

FOG = Fiber Optic Gyro, RLG = Ring Laser Gyro. Higher precision means bigger and pricier. A phone's IMU is very cheap but drifts away on its own within tens of seconds.

So it teams up with GNSS — GNSS/INS fusion

INS is very accurate short-term but drifts long-term. GNSS doesn't drift long-term but is useless the instant it's blocked. Their weaknesses are exact opposites, so combining them is unbeatable. While GNSS is visible it continuously calibrates the INS error; when GNSS is lost in a tunnel, urban canyon or under jamming, the calibrated INS smoothly bridges the gap of seconds to minutes — which is exactly what most car navs, phones and self-driving cars actually do.

While GNSS is present

The true position estimates and corrects the INS bias and drift (a Kalman filter).

The moment GNSS drops

The just-calibrated INS continues dead reckoning. For short blockages the error is tiny.

A car nav holding position through a tunnel relies on this fusion plus help from wheel odometry. See also GNSS Alternatives (overview).
