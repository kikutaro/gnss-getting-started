    Why multiple frequencies? Radio waves are delayed by the ionosphere, and the delay depends on frequency. Measure the same satellite on two frequencies (L1+L2 or L1+L5) and the difference reveals the ionospheric delay itself, which can then be cancelled — the main reason dual-frequency receivers are so accurate.

    Why L5 resists multipath: ranging signals are chip streams, and a receiver can separate a direct wave from a reflection when their arrival difference exceeds one chip. L1 C/A chips are ~293 m long; L5 chips are 10× faster, ~29 m. So on L5, any reflection that traveled 29+ m farther is simply rejected. Switch from L1 to L5 and watch ⚠ satellites turn into "reflection rejected ✓".

    Trade-off: L1 has the most satellites; L5 is still rolling out. Switching bands reveals "unsupported" satellites and a smaller usable count.

    QZSS (Michibiki) — quasi-zenith orbit and L6/CLAS

    QZSS is Japan's GNSS. It transmits GPS-compatible signals (L1/L2/L5) plus its own L6 band carrying CLAS (Centimeter Level Augmentation Service) corrections. L6 is a broadcast channel for corrections, not a ranging signal — the simulator treats it differently when selected.

    Its signature feature is the quasi-zenith orbit: a figure-8 that keeps a satellite almost directly above Japan for hours. In the view, J01/J02 sway slowly near the zenith and are rarely blocked even in an urban canyon. Try changing the QZSS count in the Urban canyon scenario.

    How GNSS works — measuring arrival time

    GNSS satellites continuously broadcast their position and precise time. The receiver computes "travel time × speed of light" for each satellite (the pseudorange) and combines several to solve its own position. Light travels ~300,000 km/s — a 1 µs timing error is a 300 m range error.

    There are four unknowns: position (x, y, z) plus the receiver clock error, so at least four satellites are needed. Try the "Too few satellites" scenario and the verdict turns to "cannot fix".

    Multipath and urban canyons

    Signals bounced off buildings (orange paths) travel a longer way. Since the receiver converts time to distance, catching a reflection inflates the measured range — a positioning error. The +X m figures are that extra path length. The worst case is when the direct wave is blocked and only the reflection arrives (NLOS): the receiver locks onto a badly wrong distance.

    Between tall buildings (urban canyon) fewer satellites are visible and multipath multiplies — the main reason phone navigation drifts downtown. Counter-measures: elevation masking, choke-ring antennas, wideband signals like L5, more constellations, and augmentation (RTK, CLAS).
