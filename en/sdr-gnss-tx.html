Transmitting GNSS with SDR

Generating GPS / QZSS L1 C/A with LimeSDR XTRX / Mini 2.0 — designing a signal level that's "just right" for the receiver

⚠ Legal notice (read first): Radiating in the GNSS bands (L1 etc.) over the air is illegal in virtually every country (in Japan under the Radio Act; GPS repeaters are also prohibited there). Always experiment over a coax cable with generous attenuation, or inside an RF shield box. The simulator below exists precisely to design that attenuation correctly.

Workflow — build and replay L1 C/A with gps-sdr-sim

The classic recipe: ① grab broadcast ephemeris (a RINEX brdc file) from IGS → ② generate an I/Q file for any position/time: gps-sdr-sim -e brdcXXX.24n -l 35.68,139.77,50 -s 2600000 → ③ replay it through the SDR with LimeSuite (limeplayer) or SoapySDR. The receiver believes it is standing at Tokyo Station.

QZSS is L1 C/A-compatible with GPS (PRN 193+), so capable simulators generate QZSS satellites in the same framework. A ~2.6 Msps sample rate covers the C/A main lobe (±1 MHz) — easy for a LimeSDR Mini 2.0 or XTRX.

Legit uses: receiver bring-up, timing tests, jamming/spoofing robustness research. Deceiving someone else's receiver is a crime. Your own device, on a closed path, always.

Why 130 dB of attenuation? — what's wrong with "too strong"

0 dBm out of an SDR is 10¹³ times (ten trillion times) the real satellite's −130 dBm. Feed that straight in and: ① the receiver's LNA/AGC saturates and works worse, not better; ② even the leakage jams every real GNSS receiver nearby (car navs, phones, cell-site timing!); ③ you may physically damage the front-end. So: turn TX gain down AND chain fixed attenuators (20/30 dB units) to land at −120…−135 dBm.

Judge the result by C/N₀ on the receiver: real-satellite-like is mid-40s dB-Hz; pinned at high-50s means too hot. It's exactly the link-budget thinking from dB & Signal Power, applied to your bench.

Bench tips — where people get bitten

DC block is mandatory: GNSS receivers put 3–5 V bias on the antenna port to power active antennas. Connect that straight to an SDR TX port and the bias flows into it — always insert a DC block.

Phones need a shield box: phones have internal antennas, so no cable option. Legal testing means radiating faintly inside an RF-shielded enclosure.

Mind the time: a receiver that locks onto a past-dated simulation jumps its clock into the past. Keep GNSS-time-synced equipment (PCs, timing gear) away from the test path.

Consider starting with RX: before building a TX bench, receiving real L1 with an RTL-SDR + GNSS-SDR teaches the whole receive chain hands-on.
